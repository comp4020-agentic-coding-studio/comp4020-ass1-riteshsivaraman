Gravity changes the colour of light.

Not by bending it, or blocking it, or slowing it down. Light leaves a heavy object at one colour and arrives at another — and if the object is heavy enough, it arrives at no colour you can see at all.

What just happened

Climbing out of gravity costs light something

A colour is a wavelength: the distance between one crest of a light wave and the next. Short wavelengths look violet and blue, long ones look orange and red, and either side of that narrow band your eye registers nothing at all.

Light escaping a massive object has to climb out of its gravity. It can't slow down — light always travels at the same speed — so it pays the toll the only other way it can: its wave stretches. The crests spread further apart. The colour slides toward red.

That's the whole idea. Stronger gravity, longer wavelength. Push it far enough and the wave stretches past red, out of the visible band entirely, and the star goes dark — not because it stopped shining, and not because it got dimmer, but because what it's shining is no longer something an eye can catch.

z
the redshift — how much longer the wave got, as a fraction
M, R
the object's mass and radius
G, c
the gravitational constant and the speed of light

The slider above is the quantity 2GM ⁄ Rc²: mass and radius only ever appear together, so one control really is enough. At 1 that denominator hits zero and the redshift becomes infinite — that's the event horizon, and it's why the slider stops at 0.95.

Why it matters

Colour is how we weigh things we can never visit

Nobody has been to Sirius B. Nobody has stood on a neutron star. But light from both arrives here carrying a record of the gravity it escaped, written into its wavelength — and that shift is measurable from a telescope on Earth.

The same effect runs the satellite navigation in your pocket. Clocks on GPS satellites sit higher in Earth's gravity than you do, so they tick faster. Left uncorrected, the errors would accumulate to kilometres in a day.

It's a small idea with a long reach: gravity is written into the colour of light, and once you can read it, you can weigh a star you'll never touch.
